If money feels like a fog, you're not behind — you just can't see the mountain yet.

Most of us were handed plenty of financial advice and almost no map.

Save more. Invest. Build an emergency fund. All good advice — but where does each dollar actually go, and in what order? Without a picture, it blurs into a low background hum of "I should probably be doing more." That hum is exhausting, and it's the real source of money stress for most people. Not the math. The fog.

This guide hands you the map. It's the same framework NorthReserve turns into a mountain you can see: four reservoirs, each with one clear job, funded in a sensible order. Once you can see where your money lives, the next move stops being a mystery — and that's where confidence comes from.

The big idea

Your money, as a mountain.

Picture your financial life as a mountain, with water — your money — collecting in four reservoirs at different heights. A healthy reservoir sits full and solid. An underfunded one looks thin, and you can spot it long before it becomes a crisis.

The goal is never to fill all four at once. It's to build from the base up, so the whole mountain grows steadier over time. Each reservoir has a distinct purpose, and mixing them up is what makes money feel chaotic. Keep them separate and the picture gets calm fast.

The climb

Build from the base up.

There's a natural order to filling your reservoirs — each one makes the next easier to reach.

  • The Buffer — a small everyday cushion so timing never trips you up.
  • The Emergency Reservoir — your solid base for life's genuine surprises.
  • Sinking Funds — money set aside for the costs you already know are coming.
  • Retirement — the long, steady climb toward the summit.

One exception worth knowingIf your employer matches retirement contributions, grab that match early — even before the rest of the climb. It's free money and an instant, guaranteed return. We'll flag exactly where it fits in the waterfall at the end.

Reservoir 01

The Buffer

Everyday breathing room

A small cushion that lives right in your checking account, so the gap between bills and paydays never causes an overdraft or a white-knuckle week. Most "money stress" isn't about the big picture at all — it's those few days before payday when the balance gets thin. A buffer quietly ends that.

How much One pay cycle of expenses, or a flat $500–$1,000 to start. Enough that you never watch your balance approach zero.
Where it lives In checking, or a linked savings you can transfer instantly. This one is about access, not growth.
Watch out for Treating your whole checking balance as spendable. Name the buffer and mentally fence it off.
Your next step Pick your buffer number. If you're not there yet, this is reservoir #1 — start here.
Reservoir 02

The Emergency Reservoir

Your solid base

Money set aside for true emergencies only — a job loss, a medical bill, the car that dies on a Tuesday. This is the difference between a setback and a spiral. With a base, a surprise is an inconvenience. Without one, the same surprise becomes debt that takes months or years to climb back out of.

How much 3–6 months of essential expenses — not income. Just the must-pays: housing, food, utilities, insurance, minimum debt payments.
Where it lives A separate high-yield savings account. Close enough to reach in a day, far enough that you won't dip in for a sale.
Watch out for Sizing it off your income, or leaving it in checking where it quietly gets spent. Keep it separate and named.
Your next step Calculate one month of essentials. That number is your first milestone — then build toward three, then six.
Add up only your essential monthly expenses.
× 3  =  your first full target   |   × 6  =  a fully solid base
Reservoir 03

Sinking Funds

The known climbs ahead

Money you set aside monthly for expenses you know are coming but that don't hit every month — car repairs and eventual replacement, insurance premiums, the holidays, annual subscriptions, home maintenance. These aren't emergencies; they're certainties we keep treating as surprises. A sinking fund turns a $1,200 "out of nowhere" cost into a $100-a-month non-event.

How much For each known cost, divide the yearly amount by 12 and save that much monthly. Add them up — that's your contribution.
Where it lives A savings account, often at the same bank as your base, but tracked separately by purpose so the two never blur.
Watch out for Lumping these in with your emergency fund, then "borrowing" from your base for Christmas. Keep every purpose its own line.
Your next step List your three biggest irregular costs and divide each by 12. That's your starting monthly number.
Car repairs $1,200/yr ÷ 12 = $100/mo
Holidays $600/yr ÷ 12 = $50/mo
Insurance $900/yr ÷ 12 = $75/mo

Reservoir 04

Retirement

The long climb

The summit. Money invested for your future self in tax-advantaged accounts. Time is the most powerful tool you have here, and it only works in one direction — small, steady contributions made now outclimb much larger ones made later. The hardest part isn't the amount. It's starting.

How much A common target is around 15% of gross income — but start where you can. Even 1% begun today beats 15% "someday." Nudge it up with every raise.
Where it lives Tax-advantaged accounts (like a 401(k), IRA, or Roth), invested rather than sitting in cash. The account type matters more than picking perfect investments early.
Watch out for Waiting until you "have more money." The cost of waiting is the one expense you can never earn back.
Your next step Check whether you're getting your full employer match. If not, that's the single highest-return move on the entire mountain.
Employer match = an instant, guaranteed return.
Contribute at least enough to get the full match before anything past your starter buffer.
Putting it together

Where does my next dollar go?

When you have an extra dollar and aren't sure where it belongs, walk down this list and stop at the first "no." That's your answer — every time.

Do I have my starter buffer ($500–$1,000)?
No → fund the Buffer first.
Is any high-interest debt (like credit cards) on fire?
Yes → attack it next. High-interest debt outranks everything below it.
Am I getting my full employer retirement match?
No → contribute just enough to capture the whole match. Free money.
Do I have one month of essentials in my Emergency Reservoir?
No → build the base to one month.
Are my Sinking Funds covering my known yearly costs?
No → top them up so certainties stop becoming emergencies.
Is my Emergency Reservoir at 3–6 months?
No → keep filling the base.
Am I at roughly 15% toward Retirement?
No → raise your contribution a little.

Everything past that is acceleration — extra investing, paying down lower-interest debt faster, funding bigger goals. But the first "no" is always where today's dollar belongs.

The view from here

The goal was never to do everything at once.

It was to always know the next step. Fund the buffer. Capture the match. Build the base. Cover what's coming. Climb toward the summit. One reservoir at a time, the whole mountain grows steadier — and so do you. That's the quiet power of having a map instead of a fog.
